Hangul is a system, not an alphabet to memorise

Sejong designed these shapes in 1443 to be learnable in a morning. Consonants are pictures of your mouth. Vowels are built from three strokes. Adding a stroke adds a puff of air; doubling a letter tightens it. Learn the seven groups below in order and the other thirty-odd letters come almost free.

Blocks, not letters

Korean stacks letters into square syllable blocks. Reading fluently means seeing the block, not decoding its parts. Build one below, or work across the full grid — every one of the 399 combinations has real recorded audio.

받침 — the closing consonant

The single biggest stumbling block for self-taught readers. Twenty-seven different letters can sit at the bottom of a block, but they only make seven sounds. And none of them are released — you form the shape and stop, without letting a vowel leak out after it.

Why it isn’t said how it’s spelled

Korean spelling preserves the meaning of a word, then seven rules reshape it in the mouth. Once you know them, the gap between the page and the sound closes. Play both sides of each example and listen for the shift.

Verbs, and how they bend

Korean verbs carry the tense, the politeness and often the whole sentence. Every one of them ends in 다 in the dictionary, and everything else is built by swapping that ending. Learn the seven rules below and 170 verbs become a few thousand usable forms.

Putting it together

Korean marks each part of a sentence with a particle rather than by where it sits, and the verb always comes last. Once that clicks, sentences stop being word order to memorise and become slots to fill.
